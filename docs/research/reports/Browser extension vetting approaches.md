# Vet every version, not every extension once

Build it, but not as another risk score. The stores review an extension when it is submitted or updated and then stop watching. Native Chrome, Edge and Firefox policies are good at enforcing a decision and weak at making one. The independent tools that did real analysis were bought by large security platforms during 2026. What a small IT team still lacks is a tool that turns "a user wants extension X" into an explained yes or no in minutes, records why, and raises a flag when X changes owner, permissions or code. The best way to vet a CRX is a static pipeline that fits on Vercel: fetch, verify, unpack, then check the manifest, code patterns, endpoints, libraries and known-bad lists. Version diffing is the control that would have caught most real incidents, and dynamic sandboxing is an optional deeper tier that needs its own servers. A 1-100 score is the wrong headline. The evidence favours a three-way verdict (Approve, Review, Deny), a short tier on each of a few separate axes, and the points math underneath. Reputation matters in an inverted way. Ratings, badges and user counts barely separate malware from benign extensions, popular extensions are the most valuable to buy or hijack, and the strong signals are publisher history and change events. Every approval should therefore be tied to one version and one publisher snapshot, and should expire when either changes.

## The gap is assessment and change watch, not enforcement

The stores' structural weakness is **point-in-time review**. Chrome Web Store review combines automated and manual checks, looks harder at new developers, broad host access and "significant code changes", and re-reviews each update, but nothing in it watches behaviour after approval ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/webstore/review-process)). Koi Security put it bluntly: "Marketplaces review extensions at submission. They don't watch what happens after approval" ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/12/shadypanda-turns-popular-browser.html)). Manifest V3's ban on remotely hosted code is the main structural defence on Chrome and Edge, but it still allows remote JSON config, remote web services and `eval` inside sandboxed iframes ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/develop/migrate/improve-security)). Attackers live in those gaps. Wladimir Palant documented 63 MV3 extensions that injected server-supplied HTML, ran fetched script through `window.eval.call` in page context, or used `declarativeNetRequest` rules to strip CSP headers from every site ([Palant](https://palant.info/2025/01/20/malicious-extensions-circumvent-googles-remote-code-ban/)).

The track record follows from that design. A Stanford and CISPA study of the Chrome Web Store (Jul 2020 to Feb 2023) counted **280M + 63M + 3M = 346M installs** of malware, policy-violating and vulnerable extensions over three years, with malware staying live **about 380 days** on average ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/06/23/google_chrome_web_store_vetting/); [arXiv 2406.12710](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12710)). Google's answer that **under 1% of 2024 installs** contained malware does not contradict it: a small share at Chrome's scale is still a very large number of users ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/06/23/google_chrome_web_store_vetting/)). Edge is no cleaner. Microsoft itself took down 119 Edge extensions with about 2.6M users in June 2026 ([Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/06/119-edge-extensions-promised-useful-tools-instead-downloaded-malware)). Firefox AMO deployed a crypto-drainer detector in June 2025, and about 150 fake wallet add-ons got past it within two months ([BleepingComputer](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/wave-of-150-crypto-draining-extensions-hits-firefox-add-on-store/amp/)).

Native enterprise controls are strong at enforcement. Chrome's `ExtensionSettings` policy supports allow, block and force-install by ID, blocking by permission, `runtime_blocked_hosts` (up to 100 hosts) to keep extensions off sensitive domains, `minimum_version_required`, and `override_update_url` for self-hosted updates ([Chrome Enterprise Help](https://support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/9867568)). Edge uses the same JSON plus a few Edge-only fields ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/deployedge/microsoft-edge-manage-extensions-ref-guide)), and Firefox 153 added `blocked_permissions` and runtime host controls, though its `blocked_permissions` does not cover host permissions such as `<all_urls>` ([Firefox admin docs](https://firefox-admin-docs.mozilla.org/reference/policies/extensionsettings/)). Assessment is the weak part. Chrome's free request workflow lets a user click Request and an admin choose Force install, Allow or Block per organisational unit. The documented flow has **no justification field and no risk score in the request view** ([Chrome Enterprise Help](https://support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/10405494?hl=en)), and requests only work once everything else is blocked ([Secure Annex](https://secureannex.com/blog/extension-request-workflows/)). Chrome's only built-in scoring comes from Spin.AI, shown separately in the Extension Usage Report ([Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/chrome-enterprise/boost-productivity-and-security-with-the-new-chrome-web-store-for-enterprises)). Edge's "Extension monitoring" page (GA June 2026) promises to help "evaluate risk" without saying how ([MC1393800](https://mc.merill.net/message/MC1393800)). Firefox has no cloud console at all.

The commercial market has thinned out at the small-team end. In 2026 Palo Alto Networks agreed to buy Koi (about $400M reported), Akamai bought LayerX (about $205M), Zscaler bought SquareX, CrowdStrike agreed to buy Seraphic, and Socket bought Secure Annex ([KuppingerCole](https://www.kuppingercole.com/blog/care/palo-alto-networks-acquires-koi-securing-the-agentic-endpoint); [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/akamai-to-acquire-ai-and-browser-security-firm-layerx-for-205-million/); [Techzine](https://www.techzine.eu/news/security/138555/to-protect-browsers-as-the-digital-frontline-zscalers-buys-squarex); [CrowdStrike IR](https://ir.crowdstrike.com/news-releases/news-release-details/crowdstrike-acquire-seraphic-turning-any-browser-secure); [CIO Influence](https://cioinfluence.com/machine-learning/socket-acquires-secure-annex-to-expand-extension-security-across-browsers-and-developer-tools/)). CRXcavator, the free API-first scorer, has been gone since about 2024 ([Am I Being Pwned](https://amibeingpwned.com/blog/crxcavator-alternatives)). What is left splits into three groups. Free single lookups (Spin.AI, ExtensionPedia, Koidex, Am I Being Pwned) give opaque scores and serve as sales funnels. Suite add-ons score mostly on permissions and need products small teams often lack: Microsoft Defender Vulnerability Management is Windows-only and sits outside Business Premium ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-vulnerability-management/tvm-browser-extensions)), and CrowdStrike needs the Falcon agent. The third group is sales-led platforms; Spin.AI's purchases on Vendr run **$2,880 to $59,140 a year, median $14,688** ([Vendr](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/spin-ai)).

The case against building deserves weight. Chrome Enterprise Core is free and already shows a Spin.AI number next to an approve button. One practitioner notes that extension vetting matters little unless the company also manages browsers and devices ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37355122)). The research found no evidence of what small teams will pay, and Google or the acquirers could ship something better for free. The conclusion is that **a score alone is a commodity**. The value is in the job around it: an explained verdict, a decision record, policy output and change alerts. One estimate puts manual review at 15 to 30 minutes per extension ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/nexgendata/crxcavator-is-dead-heres-how-to-audit-chrome-extensions-in-2026-3n7m)). For a team handling 20 requests a month at 20 minutes each, that is 20 x 20 = 400 minutes, about 6.7 hours a month, with no re-review of anything already approved.

## A static pipeline catches mechanisms, version diffs catch the attacks

Getting the package is the first fragile step. Chrome and Edge packages come from update-service URLs that are widely used but undocumented. The Chrome pattern is `clients2.google.com/service/update2/crx?...&x=id%3D<ID>%26uc` ([PowerShell Gallery](https://powershellgallery.com/packages/CRX/0.2.0/Content/Private/Get-CRXUpdateUrl.ps1)), and Edge's is `edge.microsoft.com/extensionwebstorebase/v1/crx` ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-gb/microsoft-edge/extensions-chromium/developer-guide/alternate-distribution-options)). Reports conflict on whether Google now blocks automated downloads ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/answers/questions/1153553/how-can-i-download-a-crx-using-clients2-google-com)), so the first engineering task is to test this from Vercel's network. Firefox is easier: Mozilla's AMO v5 API documents addon and version endpoints that return a file URL, hash and permission lists without authentication, though v5 "can change at any time" ([AMO API docs](https://mozilla.github.io/addons-server/topics/api/addons.html)). The stores serve only the current version, so **the app must archive every package it sees** if it ever wants a diff.

CRX3 is a protobuf header of signed key proofs followed by a plain zip ([Chromium crx3.proto](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/e9b1018/components/crx_file/crx3.proto)). That makes it parseable and verifiable in pure TypeScript: decode the header, check the RSA-PSS or ECDSA proofs with WebCrypto, confirm the extension ID derives from the developer key, then unzip with limits on size, file count and path traversal. Chromium's verifier also checks a Google publisher proof on store packages ([crx_verifier.h](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/HEAD/components/crx_file/crx_verifier.h)).

Analysis then runs in three layers.

| Layer | What to check | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Manifest | permissions and their combinations, host patterns, `optional_*`, content-script reach (`all_frames`, `document_start`, `world: MAIN`), `externally_connectable`, `web_accessible_resources`, CSP, non-store `update_url` | Capability. Chrome shows **no install warning** for `cookies`, `scripting` or `webRequest`, whose power depends on host access ([Chrome permissions list](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/reference/permissions-list)) |
| Code | `eval`, `new Function`, string timers, remote `import()` or script tags, `innerHTML` fed by fetch, `updateDynamicRules` with fetched arrays, DNR rules removing CSP or X-Frame-Options, `cookies.getAll`, `tabs.query` for `chrome://extensions`, obfuscation (entropy, `_0x` identifiers, `atob` into eval) | Mechanism. These are the patterns Palant's 63 extensions used ([Palant](https://palant.info/2025/01/20/malicious-extensions-circumvent-googles-remote-code-ban/)) |
| Composition | vulnerable libraries via retire.js, extracted domains with registration age and abuse.ch reputation, tracking and bandwidth SDKs, code similarity to known-bad, ID lookup against public malicious lists | Context. About a third of store extensions ship a library with a known vulnerability ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2024/06/23/google_chrome_web_store_vetting/)) |

Mozilla's addons-linter, the only maintained open-source linter, supplies reusable rule names such as `NO_IMPLIED_EVAL` and `MANIFEST_CSP_UNSAFE_EVAL` ([addons-linter](https://mozilla.github.io/addons-linter/)). The other open-source tools are dated: Tarnish is "un-polished", ExtAnalysis last shipped around 2020, and DoubleX is a 2021 research prototype ([ecosyste.ms](https://awesome.ecosyste.ms/projects/github.com%2Fmandatoryprogrammer%2Ftarnish); [GitHub](https://www.github.com/Tuhinshubhra/ExtAnalysis/releases); [CISPA](https://cispa.de/en/research/publications/77765-doublex-statically-detecting-vulnerable-data-flows-in-browser-extensions-at-scale)). For known-bad lookups, the MIT-licensed MalExt Sentry list held **about 6,989 IDs** as of 6 Oct 2026 ([IT-Connect](https://www.it-connect.tech/malext-open-source-database-lists-nearly-7000-malicious-chrome-extensions/)). Do not build on VirusTotal. Its free API forbids commercial use ([Wazuh docs](https://documentation.wazuh.com/current/user-manual/capabilities/virustotal-scan/about.html)), and researchers found it performs poorly on malicious extensions ([arXiv 2509.21590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21590)).

The honest limit of static analysis is that it finds **capability and mechanism, not payload**. In the 2026 QuickLens case, malicious JavaScript was fetched from a server every five minutes and run through a hidden image `onload` handler. As Secure Annex's John Tuckner put it, "The actual malicious code never appears in the extension's source files" ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/chrome-extension-turns-malicious-after.html)). A good static engine still flags "fetches remote data and executes it on every page", which is reason enough to deny. Lab classifiers reach about 98% accuracy but flagged over 1,000 real 2023 extensions as likely malicious and decay with "strong concept drift" ([arXiv 2509.21590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.21590)), so hand-written, explainable rules suit a small team better than a trained model.

Version diffing is the highest-value control a small team can automate. Cyberhaven's malicious v24.10.4 came from a phished developer account and was live from 01:32 UTC on 25 Dec 2024 to 02:50 UTC on 26 Dec, about 25 hours ([LayerX](https://layerxsecurity.com/blog/attack-campaign-targeting-browser-extensions)). ShadyPanda's extensions were benign from 2018-19 until a mid-2024 update made five of them fetch and run remote JavaScript every hour ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/12/shadypanda-turns-popular-browser.html)). LayerX saw malicious features added **6 to 12 months** after first publication ([LayerX](https://layerxsecurity.com/?p=4717)). Every one passed store review as an update. A diff should rank changes in this order: `update_url`, new permissions or hosts, new content-script reach, `externally_connectable`, CSP changes, new network domains (especially newly registered ones), new dynamic-code sinks, new obfuscated files, and a large code jump in a minor version.

Dynamic analysis catches runtime payloads but belongs in a later phase. It means loading the extension into real Chrome with honey pages and seeded fake sessions while capturing network traffic, which is the method of the Hulk research system ([USENIX Security 2014](https://sites.cs.ucsb.edu/%7Evigna/publications/2014_USENIX_Hulk.pdf)). Attackers now wait days or fire on a fraction of page loads, and Microsoft's StegoAd sleeper ran its next stage on **about 10% of installs** ([Malwarebytes](https://www.malwarebytes.com/blog/news/2026/06/119-edge-extensions-promised-useful-tools-instead-downloaded-malware)). Remote config can also serve clean responses to datacenter IPs. A cheap middle ground is to extract the config endpoints statically and fetch them on a schedule.

LLMs help as a summary layer, never as the detector. Koi's engine was "LLM-first" and compared stated purpose with actual behaviour ([Koi blog](https://www.koi.ai/blog)). Attackers are already planting prompt injection and refusal-bait comments in packages to derail AI scanners, as in Zscaler's June 2026 PyPI wave ([Infosecurity Magazine](https://infosecurity-magazine.com/news/malware-ai-detection-npm-package)). One summary of Cloudflare testing reported AI review detection falling from 67.3% to 53.3% when deceptive comments made up under 1% of a file ([Pulse](https://www.pulse.bot/cybersecurity/news/cloudflare-uncovers-promptinjection-attacks-that-fool-ai-code-review-tools-06712258-fcc4-42de-8f02-2d68ff9ee469/)). The safe design follows from that. Strip comments, send the model the deterministic findings and the store description, ask whether behaviour matches purpose, require file-and-line evidence that the app checks, and never let the model lower a rule-based flag. Treat a refusal as a finding.

## Ratings should be a verdict with the math underneath, not 1-100

A 1-100 score does not make sense as the headline. It implies 100 meaningful steps of resolution that a heuristic, mostly permission-driven model cannot support, and the industry itself collapses its numbers to 3 to 5 tiers before anyone acts. Google maps Spin.AI's 0-100 and LayerX's 0-10 into the same Low, Medium and High bands ([Chrome Enterprise Help](https://support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/10836225?hl=en)). Microsoft Defender sets an extension's risk to its single worst permission, from None to Critical, and adds: "Risk is subjective" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/defender-vulnerability-management/tvm-browser-extensions)). Numbers also confuse when their direction is unclear. Help Net Security called Spin.AI's figure a "safety score out of 100" ([Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2023/05/03/spin-ai-google/)), while Google's console treats higher as riskier (Low 1-34, High 65-100) ([Chrome Enterprise Help](https://support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/10836225?hl=en)). Google also warns that a LayerX score of 3.1 to 3.9 "might temporarily appear as medium risk", a band-edge wobble that a fine scale invites.

Permission-only scores also cry wolf. Delinea publishes the clearest model in the field: `<all_urls>` 30 points, `debugger` 30, `cookies` 15, `tabs` 10, `storage` 0, plus modifiers such as sideloaded +20, with bands Low 0-15, Medium 16-35, High 36-60 and Critical 61+ ([Delinea docs](https://docs.delinea.com/online-help/privilege-manager/computer-groups/policies/browser-extensions/bem-risk.htm)). In Delinea's own 60-extension sample, Critical was the largest tier: **19 / 60 = 32% Critical**, and (18 + 19) / 60 = 62% High or worse, mostly legitimate password managers and ad blockers. A study of 21 SOC practitioners found that this kind of false-positive load leads to desensitisation and mistrust ([USENIX Security 2022](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity22/presentation/alahmadi)). CRXcavator had the same flaw: its unweighted, uncapped sum measured "risk surface, not malicious intent" ([Am I Being Pwned](https://amibeingpwned.com/blog/crxcavator-alternatives)).

Words alone do not fix this. Verbal risk labels are read more variably than numbers ([PMC9914684](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9914684/)), and vague risk information can raise anxiety without raising awareness ([Nurse et al.](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/files/6636/STAST2011_NCGL_authors_final.pdf)). Mature scoring systems answer with a label backed by visible line items. OpenSSF Scorecard warns that its aggregate "tells you nothing about what individual behaviors a repository is or is not doing" ([OpenSSF](https://github.com/ossf/scorecard/blob/main/README.md)). Mozilla calls its own grade bands "essentially arbitrary" ([mozilla/http-observatory](https://github.com/mozilla/http-observatory/blob/main/httpobs/docs/scoring.md)). CVSS v4 labels each score with which metric groups were actually assessed ([FIRST](https://www.first.org/cvss/v4.0/specification-document)). Socket splits package risk into five 0-100 axes plus typed alerts, and tools that consume it gate only on the security axes ([developer-tech.com](https://www.developer-tech.com/news/socket-security-analysis-on-npm-shield-for-supply-chains/)).

The design that follows has four parts. First, a **verdict** of Approve, Review or Deny. Second, a **tier per axis** on four axes kept separate: Capability (manifest), Code (mechanisms found), Reputation (publisher and history) and Change (what moved since the last approved version). Third, **line-item math** under each tier. Fourth, **hard-fail rules** that set the verdict whatever the points say: on a known-malicious list, remote code executed, CSP-stripping network rules, non-store `update_url`, or obfuscated code with network calls. Points add up within an axis only. Across axes the worst signal wins, so ten minor items never look like one severe one, and good reputation never dilutes a malicious-code flag. A 10M-user password manager then reads correctly as Capability High, Code Low, Reputation Low, rather than as one alarming number.

```
Verdict   Review                                    rules v3, scanned: manifest + code + listing
Capab.    High     cookies 15 + <all_urls> 30 + tabs 10 = 55       (High 36-60)
Code      Medium   1 sink: fetch -> innerHTML   (background.js:212)
Reput.    Low      publisher unchanged 3 yrs, 0 removed siblings
Change    n/a      first review
```

Three refinements keep this honest. "Not analysed" is its own visible state that routes to Review, never zero points, unlike Delinea, which gives unknown permissions 0. Each result records which analyses ran and which rule version produced it, so an approval made under rules v3 stays readable after the weights change. Thresholds are editable per organisation, because Microsoft, OWASP and CVSS all say tolerance varies, and the app should track how often reviewers override each tier. OWASP recommends tuning against expert judgement ([OWASP](https://community.owasp.org/OWASP_Risk_Rating_Methodology)). If a reviewer overrides a tier most of the time, the tier is wrong. No study directly compares 0-100, letter grades and tiers for IT approval decisions, so this design is inferred from adjacent fields and vendor practice rather than proven.

## Reputation marks the target more than it vouches for the code

Reputation matters, but mostly in the opposite direction from the one users assume. The best-documented incidents of 2024 to 2026 hit extensions that looked trustworthy by every visible measure.

| Case | Route to control | Trust signals at the time | Gap or scale |
|---|---|---|---|
| QuickLens, 2026 | Sold through broker ExtensionHub | Sibling ShotBird was Featured | Transfer 1 Feb, payload 17 Feb = 16 days ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/chrome-extension-turns-malicious-after.html)) |
| Urban VPN, 2025 | Own publisher pivot (affiliated with data broker BiScience) | Featured, about 6M users | AI chat capture from v5.5.0, disclosed in privacy policy ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/16/chrome_edge_privacy_extensions_quietly/)) |
| ShadyPanda, 2025 | Long-run sleeper | Clean Master Featured and Verified | Benign from 2018-19 to mid-2024, 4.3M users ([Hackread](https://hackread.com/shadypanda-attack-spied-chrome-edge-users/)) |
| Geco / RedDirection, 2025 | Silent update | Verified, Featured, 800+ reviews, 4.2 stars | Clean "for years", 2.3M users ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2025/07/08/browser-hijacking-campaign-infects-23m-chrome-edge-users/1236258)) |
| Cyberhaven, 2024 | Phished developer account | Verified security vendor | About 25 hours live, campaign 2.6M+ users ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/106205-widespread-cyberattack-targets-google-chrome-extensions-compromises-2-6-million-devices.html)) |
| The Great Suspender, 2020-21 | Sold | 2M+ users | Removed about 7 to 8 months after sale ([Security Affairs](https://securityaffairs.com/114272/malware/the-great-suspender-extension-malware.html)) |
| Nano Adblocker, 2020 | Sold | 300k installs | Sale 4 Oct, flagged 16 Oct = 12 days ([gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/?p=165492)) |

Counts for the Cyberhaven-era campaign vary between 33 and 40 extensions and 2.6M to 3.7M users depending on the source ([Spin.AI](https://spin.ai/blog/cyberhaven-attack-puts-more-users-at-risk/)). The pattern does not. There are three routes to the same outcome: a sale, an account takeover, and the original publisher's own monetisation pivot. Ownership-change detection catches only the first, and only when listing fields change.

Buying an extension is cheap. Tuckner bought one for **$50**, transferred it for a **$5** fee through Google's support form, and pushed new code "within hours" using permissions it already held ([CyberScoop](https://cyberscoop.com/browser-extension-sales-permissions-hidden-threat/)). Transfers keep the ID, code and users, and users are reportedly not told ([Chromium extensions group](https://groups.google.com/a/chromium.org/g/chromium-extensions/c/-x6LrspgArc)). QuickLens was listed for sale two days after it was published ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/chrome-extension-turns-malicious-after.html)). Developers describe a steady stream of buy offers, including buyers who want account credentials rather than a formal transfer, which leaves no visible ownership change at all ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=39620060)). Monetisation SDKs bypass ownership entirely. The Mellowtel scraping library turned up in 245 extensions with about 900,000 users and also stripped CSP headers ([PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/2845330/hundreds-of-chrome-extensions-create-a-web-scraping-botnet.html)).

The store's visible signals barely separate good from bad. In the Stanford data, median ratings were **about 5 for benign versus 4.997 for malware**. Mean user counts were **about 27k for malware versus 11k for benign**, so a large install base raises the stakes rather than lowering the risk. Longevity proves little when malware averaged 380 days live and one sample lasted 3,105 days ([arXiv 2406.12710](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12710v1)). Fake reviews cost **about $1 each**, and Google does not verify them ([Frisbie](https://mattfrisbie.substack.com/p/lets-buy-some-fake-chrome-extension)). Badges are one-time manual checks of quality and identity ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/webstore/discovery)), they survived every transfer above, and nothing suggests they are re-checked on update.

The strong signals concern the publisher and change. Developers with one malware extension publish **about 4.9 more** on average, and 321 code-similarity clusters contain nothing but problem extensions ([arXiv 2406.12710](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.12710v1)). With 59.5% of extensions never updated, a long-dormant extension that suddenly updates is notable. A publisher change (name, contact email, website domain) followed by an update within about 30 days is the strongest combined flag. The QuickLens transfer showed up as a new `.top` contact address on the listing ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2026/03/chrome-extension-turns-malicious-after.html)). The open-source tool "under-new-management" already polls developer metadata hourly to catch this ([Hacker News](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=39620060)). A privacy policy's existence means little; Urban VPN's disclosed the AI chat collection. Its content, such as data-broker ties or "marketing analytics" sharing, is what to parse.

The practical answer is to **tie each approval to a version and a publisher snapshot**. Managed Chrome can pin a force-installed extension to a version per organisational unit, or take updates only from a self-hosted CRX through `override_update_url` ([Chrome Enterprise Help](https://support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/7532015?hl=en)). `minimum_version_required` forces devices past a known-bad build ([Chrome for Developers](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/extensions/develop/concepts/extensions-update-lifecycle)). Pinning would have blocked the Cyberhaven, Geco, QuickLens and Clean Master updates, if the pinned version was clean. It costs missed security fixes and a re-review queue, and it does nothing against a pinned version that already fetches and runs server code. That is why the remote-code rule has to be a hard fail at first review. Edge's pinning specifics were not confirmed in this research.

## Build the request desk first, then the watcher

The product is a request desk with an analysis engine behind it, not a scanner with a form in front. The order below puts the most-used, cheapest, all-Vercel pieces first and saves the infrastructure-heavy pieces for later.

| Phase | Build | Runs on |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Request desk | Intake by store URL or ID plus a business-justification field; fetch, verify CRX3 and unpack safely; manifest rules with Delinea-style points; MalExt and other ID-list lookups; verdict page with axis tiers and math; decision record (who, when, why, rules version); export of `ExtensionSettings` JSON for Chrome and Edge and `policies.json` for Firefox, including pinned versions and `runtime_blocked_hosts` for SSO, email and finance domains | Next.js on Vercel, Postgres, Vercel Blob for packages keyed by ID + version + SHA-256 |
| 2. Code engine | AST rules (acorn or Babel) for sinks, remote-code mechanisms and DNR header stripping; obfuscation heuristics; retire.js; domain extraction with RDAP age and abuse.ch reputation; SDK fingerprints; store-listing snapshot of publisher name, email, website and policy URL; Firefox via the AMO API | Vercel functions (pure JS) |
| 3. Watcher | Vercel Cron polls each approved ID for a new version, archives and diffs it, re-scores, and moves the extension to a re-review queue with the delta shown (`+cookies, +<all_urls>; Capab. 22 -> 48`); alerts on publisher change, new version after long dormancy, and store removal | Vercel Cron, functions, Blob |
| 4. Second opinion | LLM purpose-vs-behaviour summary on changed files only, with comment stripping and evidence checking; code-similarity hashing against known-bad corpora | Vercel functions calling a model API; corpus storage |
| 5. Deep tier | Dynamic sandbox (Chrome for Testing with the extension loaded, honey pages, network capture, time skew, scheduled fetches of extracted config endpoints); optional DoubleX and Semgrep; fleet import from the Chrome usage report or Defender API | Separate container or VM workers with a job queue |

Phases 1 to 3 fit Vercel serverless because the work is pure JavaScript on packages of a few megabytes. Four things need infrastructure beyond it. Dynamic analysis needs Chrome with extensions loaded, about 1 to 2 GB of RAM per instance and runs of 30 to 120 seconds or longer, which outgrows function limits, so it belongs on a container or VM worker. DoubleX (Python) and Semgrep (Python and OCaml) cannot run in a Node function. A similarity corpus and store-wide crawling grow into a real data job. Downloads may also be blocked from cloud IP ranges, which is untested and could force a fetch proxy before Phase 1 ships. Long LLM reviews of large extensions should go through a queue rather than a request-bound function, even if they stay on Vercel.

Several product rules fall out of the research. Make version and publisher snapshot part of every approval, and let approvals expire on change rather than on a calendar. Keep "not analysed" visible and route it to Review. Never use store badges, ratings or user counts as a pass signal; show them as context. Log the rule that fired on every automatic decision. Track override rates per tier to calibrate. Build a free public single-extension lookup only if it feeds the paid workflow, since the free lookups already set the price of a bare score at zero. Two practical cautions apply: the Chrome and Edge download endpoints and store-listing scraping are undocumented and may break or conflict with store terms, which were not reviewed, and the app's value to a customer depends on that customer actually blocking unapproved extensions through policy.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question. The difference between a safe and an unsafe extension is less about what it is today than about who controls it and what it is allowed to become, and the industry's tooling still mostly scores a snapshot. Every major incident of the last two years was an update to something already trusted, so the defensible unit of approval is not "extension X" but "extension X, version N, publisher P, with no remote-execution mechanism". Phrased that way, the request desk and the change watcher are one feature, and a small team can build it on serverless infrastructure.

The open risks are commercial and operational rather than technical: no evidence yet of what small IT teams will pay, undocumented download endpoints, and large vendors that could move down-market after their 2026 acquisitions. Testing the download path from Vercel, and putting the Phase 1 desk in front of a handful of real IT reviewers to measure time saved and override rates, would settle the biggest unknowns faster than further research.
